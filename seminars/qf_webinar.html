Dr. Boris Klebanov | 4 September | Learning from the Most Relevant Past: Kernel-Based Risk and Prediction

Speaker: Dr. Boris Klebanov

Title: Learning from the Most Relevant Past: Kernel-Based Risk and Prediction

Date/Time: 4 September, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: We introduce a kernel-weighted framework that adapts empirical estimates to the current state. The core mechanism is simple: replace uniform observation weights with similarity-based kernel weights. In the first part, we apply this to the classical Conditional Value-at-Risk optimization of Rockafellar and Uryasev. The resulting risk measure remains convex, coherent, and—for linear losses—a linear program, preserving full tractability. We prove asymptotic consistency under standard mixing conditions. In the second part, we extend the same kernel weighting to jointly estimate a conditional mean vector and covariance matrix, and introduce the Effective Predictor Size as a diagnostic of estimation reliability. This yields a unified, transparent framework for adaptive risk and distributional inference.

Seeking to apply rigorous mathematical tools to real-world problems, Dr. Klebanov transitioned from pure mathematics to quantitative methods in economics and finance, with applications to risk management and forecasting. Dr. Michael Isichenko | 18 September | Price Impact and Paradoxes of Inelastic Stock Market

Speaker: Dr. Michael Isichenko

Title: Price Impact and Paradoxes of Inelastic Stock Market

Date/Time: 18 September, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: We discuss phenomenological models of inelastic stock market response to aggregate money flows, including a model with a semi-permanent price impact. The conservation of money in financial transactions means that the aggregate cash investments go through rather than into the stock market, while not reaching the economics of stock issuers in any significant way but affecting stock prices via forces of supply and demand. Market microstructure embedded in a complex graph of money flow implies that the net flow of funds sets overall stock prices via price impact mechanics rather than economics. We argue that the overall secondary stock market capitalization is the result of limited supply of shares and growing demand by households, primarily via large institutions such as mutual and pension funds, and somewhat technical inelastic effects, rather than reflective of the underlying economic activity or enterprise value of the stock issuers. This observation makes long-term equity investment an economic paradox and raises further questions. The views expressed in this presentation are solely the author’s and do not necessarily represent the views of his employer.

Dan diBartolomeo | 2 October | Variety is the Spice of Active Management (as well as Life)

Speaker: Dan diBartolomeo, President, Northfield Investments

Title: Variety is the Spice of Active Management (as well as Life)

Date/Time: 2 October, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: The effectiveness of any non-passive investment strategy, including tax aware strategies, is dependent on four aspects of the problem. The first is the skill of the investor. The second is the set of opportunities that are available given the definition of the strategy. The third is the cost of transactions. The fourth is the extent to which constraints limit the opportunities available. In this presentation, we will focus on the second of the four attributes. In general, the opportunity set is defined by the cross-sectional dispersion (i.e. “variety”) of the security returns for the universe allowed under the strategy. First, we will review how variety is the key ingredient to most active strategies. In addition, we can think of tax aware strategies such as tax-loss harvesting as options, in which variety is the “volatility of the underlying.” We will next consider how we can correct for the usual (but generally incorrect) presumption that the cross-section of returns is normally distributed. We will introduce formulae that can be used to predict the magnitude of the cross-sectional dispersion measure and the ex-ante implications for classic equity factors such as “value” and “momentum”. Empirical data from late 2020 through late 2024 will be used to illustrate the various uses to which the variety concept can be put.

Prof. Sergiy Butenko | 16 October | Risk-Controlled Wagering Portfolios: Scenario Covering, Downside Protection, and Responsible Sports-Betting Design

Speaker: Prof. Sergiy Butenko

Title: Risk-Controlled Wagering Portfolios: Scenario Covering, Downside Protection, and Responsible Sports-Betting Design

Date/Time: 16 October, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: The rapid expansion of legal sports wagering has created a large-scale consumer decision environment in which increasingly complex bets are offered through highly accessible digital platforms. We develop an optimization-based framework for sports wagering that focuses on downside protection rather than expected growth. A bettor allocates a fixed budget across straight bets and multibets while seeking to guarantee a target payout over a prescribed set of outcome scenarios and explicitly controlling exposure to unfavorable outcomes. The resulting model provides a tractable way to study tradeoffs among guaranteed payout, scenario coverage, and risk. We discuss structural properties of optimal wagering strategies, computational approaches, and extensions involving restrictions on parlays and loss probabilities. The framework also provides a basis for studying risk disclosure and responsible-betting mechanisms in increasingly complex wagering environments.

Prof. Esra Büyüktahtakın Toy | 13 November | Scenario Dominance for Mean–CVaR Multistage Stochastic Optimization

Speaker: Prof. Esra Büyüktahtakın Toy (Virginia Tech)

Title: Scenario Dominance for Mean–CVaR Multistage Stochastic Optimization

Date/Time: 13 November, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: We introduce Stage-t Scenario Dominance, a methodology for solving mean–Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) multistage stochastic mixed-integer programs. By exploiting the partial ordering of scenarios, we derive lower bounds and optimality-preserving cutting planes from scenario subproblems that retain the original nonanticipativity constraints. We further develop stage-specific dominance relationships and an aggressive cutting-plane strategy to accelerate computation. Computational experiments on mean–CVaR multistage stochastic knapsack problems demonstrate substantial improvements, closing 94.3% of the root integrality gap on average, reducing it from 2.37% to 0.10%. The aggressive cutting-plane strategy achieves a reported 72-fold average speedup. We also briefly illustrate the methodology's practical relevance through an emerald ash borer surveillance and control application.

Prof. Charles-Albert Lehalle | 6 February | Inferring Mean Field Game parameters from real data: the case of maritime traffic

Speaker: Prof. Charles-Albert Lehalle

Title: Inferring Mean Field Game parameters from real data: the case of maritime traffic

Date/Time: 6 February, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: I will present a Mean Field Game (MFG) model designed to analyze and manage maritime traffic flows at a global scale. Building on a microscopic interpretation of ships as rational agents optimizing profit and costs, we propose a mesoscopic MFG formulation in which each seaport acts as a coordinator choosing optimal routing strategies. These decisions balance transportation costs, expected commercial margins, and congestion effects at destination ports.
A key contribution of this work is a statistical framework for estimating the parameters of the game—cost components and expected profit drivers—directly from real data. Using the global ShipFix dataset of daily Dry Coal shipments (2015–2025), we show how parameter inference anchors the model in observed economic behavior and enables robust counterfactual analysis. This opens the door to economically and financially meaningful “what-if” scenarios, particularly relevant in today’s environment of extreme weather disruptions, geopolitical shocks, and volatile trade patterns.

Dr. Iro Tasitsiomi | 20 February | Virtuous or Vain? Complexity in Equity-Premium Forecasting

Speaker: Dr. Iro Tasitsiomi

Title: Virtuous or Vain? Complexity in Equity-Premium Forecasting

Date/Time: 20 February, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Location: Math Tower, Room B-148 (& Via Zoom)

Abstract: Despite rapid advances in highly complex predictive models (e.g., neural networks), parsimony and interpretability remain central in finance. Recent work using Random Fourier Features (RFFs) has reignited this tension by arguing for the “virtue of complexity” in forecasting equity premia. Critics have responded sharply, pointing to restrictive assumptions, specification choices, potential data leakage, and (for some) limited relevance for financial economics.
This talk situates the debate in the broader framework of modern statistical learning, where high-dimensional feature expansions and regularization are standard tools for prediction. Using the benchmark dataset shared across these studies, I compare ridge-based approaches—including the original RFF setup, kernel ridge regression, and shallow neural networks. I evaluate models using two metrics: the information coefficient and the Sharpe ratio of the trading strategy implied by the forecasts. The empirical analysis examines (i) how RFFs reshape the eigenspectrum of predictors, (ii) performance as a function of complexity and regularization, (iii) whether gains reflect market exposure or genuine timing skill under the original trading design, and (iv) implications for competing claims in the debate. I conclude with open questions on complexity, regularization, and robustness in noisy financial prediction.

Dr. Arun Muralidhar | 6 March | A Realistic, Heterogeneous (Principal-) Agent Asset Pricing Model: With Asset Allocation and Risk-Adjusted Performance

Speaker: Dr. Arun Muralidhar

Title: A Realistic, Heterogeneous (Principal-) Agent Asset Pricing Model: With Asset Allocation and Risk-Adjusted Performance

Date/Time: 6 March, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), based on investors/principals, maximizing their expected utility of wealth, assumed a single representative investor and a deterministic goal/liability. Subsequent heterogenous agent asset pricing models (HAPMs) were based on variations to utility functions/preferences, and market frictions, but, in reality, there are no well-specified “utility functions”, or “risk-aversion” parameters (and probably HAPMs incorrectly use “agent” instead of “participant” or “principal”). Prof. Cochrane has suggested that, “Switching to a payoff perspective may change lots of procedures in finance”, and we use his approach to propose a realistic HAPM that corrects for these challenges.
Three variations of realistic heterogeneity in markets are explored instead in this model: (i) heterogeneity of cash flows of different investment goals (retirement; kid’s education, buying a house, etc.); (ii) heterogeneity of cash flows within a goal, based on different ages of investors that impact cash flow start/end dates and duration; and (iii) heterogeneity of clearly articulated relative risk targets for delegation. The proposed model incorporates four realities of investing; (i) that investors/principals have many stochastic goals (i.e., cash flows or payoffs they desire), and that is the primary example of real-life heterogeneity; (ii) they seek to delegate to skillful agents (in the true academic sense of the word); (iii) explicitly specify risk preferences (adding in an additional layer of heterogeneity); and (iv) maximize risk-adjusted relative returns. In this HAPM, assets are priced with just two goal-replicating assets (issued by Brazil in 2023), and the absolute risk-free asset. The pair-wise equilibrium model uses observable assets and risk preferences parameters, eliminates "free parameters", and lends itself to a general equilibrium model of assets and wages too. Critical to investment practice, it provides asset pricing, asset allocations and risk-adjusted performance measures that are consistent. It offers a new pathway to a realistic HAPM.

Prof. Petter Kolm | 27 March | Identifying Patterns in Financial Markets: Extending the Statistical Jump Model for Regime Identification

Speaker: Prof. Petter Kolm

Title: Identifying Patterns in Financial Markets: Extending the Statistical Jump Model for Regime Identification

Date/Time: 27 March, 2026, 10:00-11:00 AM

Location: Via Zoom

Abstract: Regime-driven models are popular for addressing temporal patterns in both financial market performance and underlying stylized factors, wherein a regime describes periods with relatively homogeneous behavior. Recently, statistical jump models have been proposed to learn regimes with high persistence, based on clustering temporal features while explicitly penalizing jumps across regimes. In this article, we extend the jump model by generalizing the discrete hidden state variable into a probability vector over all regimes. This allows us to estimate the probability of being in each regime, providing valuable information for downstream tasks such as regime-aware portfolio models and risk management. Our model’s smooth transition from one regime to another enhances robustness over the original discrete model. We provide a probabilistic interpretation of our continuous model and demonstrate its advantages through simulations and real-world data experiments. The interpretation motivates a novel penalty term, called mode loss, which pushes the probability estimates to the vertices of the probability simplex thereby improving the model’s ability to identify regimes. We demonstrate through a series of empirical and real world tests that the approach outperforms traditional regime-switching models. This outperformance is pronounced when the regimes are imbalanced and historical data is limited, both common in financial markets.

Speaker: Mr. Slawek Smyl

Title: Context in time series forecasting with neural networks.

Date/Time: 5 September, 2025, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: Although global models are trained across many series, but typically, at one moment they “see” a single series. Often however, it is not obvious how to find such “friendly” time series (e.g. a most helpful series for a beer may turn out to be some chips), especially in larger datasets. We will describe a few possibilities how to deal with the issue.

Prof. Yao Xie | 19 September | Conformal Prediction for Time Series

Speaker: Yao Xie

Title: Conformal Prediction for Time Series

Date/Time: 19 September, 2025, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: Conformal prediction (CP) provides a powerful, distribution-free framework for constructing prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees. However, many CP methods rely on the assumption of data exchangeability, which is typically violated in time series due to temporal dependence. This has motivated a growing body of work aimed at extending CP to the time-series setting. Another computational challenge comes from handling multi-dimensional time-series. In this talk, I will review recent advances in conformal prediction for time series, including our own contributions to a general framework for constructing distribution-free prediction intervals for time series that wrap around a given black-box algorithm. The new approach relaxes the exchangeability assumption and allows for general forms of temporal and multi-variate dependence across dimensions. Theoretically, we establish marginal and conditional coverage guarantees, which comes with certain width optimality in some cases. Methodologically, we propose computationally efficient procedures based on ensemble predictors that are closely related to standard CP, yet tailored for time series.

Dr. Alexander Kreinin | 3 October | Backward Simulation of Hybrid Models

Speaker: Dr. Alexander Kreinin

Title: Backward Simulation of Hybrid Models

Date/Time: 3 October, 2025, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: Jump models and diffusion models are often used in financial and insurance modelling. In the case of pure jump processes with dependent components the simulation and calibration problems can be challenging. The first obstacle is that it is not clear how to obtain negative correlations of the Poisson processes in the natural filtration. Indeed, correlated Poisson processes in the natural filtration must have simultaneous jumps [7], [8], but in this case the correlation will be positive. However, there are many practical situations where pure jump components can be modelled by Poisson processes and, at the same time, have rather strong negative correlations that can not be ignored.

Title: Toward a General Theory of Elicitation Complexity

Date/Time: 1 Oct. 2021, 10:00-11:00 AM

Abstract: In statistics, finance, machine learning, economics, and several other domains, the question arises of which statistical functionals are elicitable, meaning they can be expressed as the minimizer of expected loss for some loss function. Several important functionals are not elicitable, however, including many risk and uncertainty measures of interest. To circumvent this negative result, several authors have proposed notions of "higher-order" elicitability, wherein a d-dimensional functional is elicited, and then condensed to recover the functional of interest. The smallest possible value of d is called the elicitation complexity. We will discuss several related definitions as part of a unifying framework, and give upper and lower bounds on elicitation complexity for a broad class of risk and uncertainty measures. The talk will conclude with several interesting open problems.

Abstract: The concept of entropy has its origins in the 19 th century in the discovery of thermodynamics (Carnot, Clausius, Kelvin) and statistical mechanics (Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs). In physics, for example, it is well known that the laws of thermodynamics can be derived from entropic methods. What might at first sight be surprising is that entropic principles can also be used to derive the dynamical laws of mechanics, both classical and quantum. The variance gamma process is a pure-jump process which is constructed by replacing the calendar time by the gamma time in a Brownian motion with drift, which makes it a time-changed Brownian motion. In the case of the Black-Merton-Scholes model, there are fast approximation methods for pricing American options, but they cannot be utilized for the variance gamma model. We develop a new fast and accurate approximation method inspired by the quadratic approximation to get rid of the time steps required in finite difference methods and simulation methods, while reducing the error by making use of a machine learning technique on pre-calculated quantities. We compare the performance of our method with those of the existing methods and show that this method is efficient and accurate for practical use.

Harvey Stein | 8 May 2020 | A Unified Framework for Default Modeling

Speaker: Harvey Stein, Bloomberg LP

Title: A Unified Framework for Default Modeling

Date/Time: 8 May 2020, Friday, 10:00 - 11:00 AM EST

Abstract: Credit risk models largely bifurcate into two classes -- the structural models and the reduced form models. Attempts have been made to reconcile the two approaches by adjusting filtrations to restrict information, but they are technically complicated and tend to approach filtration modification in an ad-hock fashion. Here we propose a reconciliation inspired by actuarial science's approach to survival analysis. We model the survival curve and hazard rate curve as stochastic processes. This puts default models in a form resembling the HJM framework for interest rates, yielding a unified framework for default modeling. Predictability of default has a simple interpretation in this framework. The framework enables us to disentangle predictability and the distribution of the default time from calibration decisions such as whether to use market prices or balance sheet information. It supplies a formal framework for combining models, yielding a simple way to define new default models.

Agostino Capponi | 15 May 2020 | Large Orders in Small Markets: On Optimal Execution with Endogenous Liquidity Supply

Speaker: Agostino Capponi, Columbia University IEOR Department

Title: Large Orders in Small Markets: On Optimal Execution with Endogenous Liquidity Supply

Date/Time: 15 May 2020, Friday, 10:00 - 11:00 AM EST

Abstract: We solve a continuous time dynamic Stackelberg game, where a large uninformed seller executes optimally, fully cognizant of the response of Cournot-competitive market makers. The game therefore endogenizes both demand and supply of liquidity. We provide closed form solutions for the value functions of market makers and large sellers, the equilibrium bid/ask price, and the execution intensity. The closed-form solution yields several insights. First, stealth trading is both privately and socially costly because market makers incur additional costs not knowing when execution ends. Second, the presence of a large seller does not unambiguously benefit other participants. Market makers benefit only if there is enough risk-absorption capacity or if the execution period is short. Other investors benefit only when the seller sells at high enough intensity. The model explains quantitatively several empirical facts: order duration and participation rate correlate negatively, and price pressure subsides before execution ends.
